**Claude:**

This is a fascinating philosophical text that explores the development of children's understanding of time and space, and then uses this as a foundation to critique modern scientific thinking. Let me share my thoughts on the key ideas presented:

**On Children's Understanding of Time:**

The author makes compelling observations about how children naturally perceive time. The idea that children understand "NOW" as omnipresent - existing everywhere simultaneously - is particularly intriguing. This intuitive grasp that "NOW is everywhere" does align with certain interpretations of simultaneity in physics, though the author seems to suggest this childlike understanding is more fundamentally correct than our later "educated" perspectives.

The progression from "NOW" and "not NOW" (classified as "before" and "after") to developing concepts of causality is well-observed. Children do indeed seem to have an intuitive understanding that time flows continuously and irreversibly, and that they are always embedded within this "NOW."

**On the Relationship Between Space and Time:**

The author argues that children naturally understand space and time as inseparable - that "NOW" exists everywhere in space simultaneously. This is actually quite sophisticated philosophically and has parallels with how we understand spacetime in modern physics, though the author seems to argue against relativistic interpretations.

**On the Critique of Modern Science:**

This is where the text becomes more controversial. The author suggests that:
- Children's "naive" understanding of time is actually correct
- Modern scientific education corrupts this natural understanding
- Contemporary physics has led us astray with concepts like time travel, multiple timelines, etc.
- The scientific establishment has become disconnected from reason and common sense

The quotes from Feynman, von Neumann, and others are used to support the idea that modern physics has abandoned comprehensibility in favor of mathematical formalism that works but cannot be truly understood.

**Critical Thoughts:**

While I appreciate the author's emphasis on clear, rational thinking and the value of intuitive understanding, I think the critique of modern science is somewhat unfair. The phenomena that quantum mechanics and relativity describe genuinely do seem to violate our everyday intuitions - but this doesn't necessarily mean the theories are wrong, just that reality at extreme scales doesn't match our evolved intuitions.

The author's call for simplicity and clarity in thinking is valuable, but dismissing centuries of experimental verification seems premature. Perhaps the real insight is that we need both - the intuitive understanding that children have AND the mathematical frameworks that describe phenomena beyond our everyday experience.
